chalk.

I founded chalk and built it in under 4 months: an AI teacher that draws the lesson while it talks you through it. Here’s how it works.

Launched 6 November 2026, on the web and the Mac · more than 140 engines

The problem
Ask an AI to explain something and you get a wall of text, or a diagram it guessed. A teacher that draws has to draw the right thing, in the right place, while it talks.
How I framed it
The model describes; code computes. The model says what the lesson needs, and chalk works out every position itself. No tool the model uses accepts a coordinate, and where a figure can be calculated, chalk’s own code calculates it.
The call
On the Mac, chalk points at the real control by its name. I threw away asking the model for a pixel position. On a test of 182 named buttons across 13 recorded screens, naming the element hit 97%; asking for a pixel position hit 59%.
The numbers
Built in under 4 months and launched on 6 November 2026, on the web and the Mac together, with more than 140 engines. Students paid in the first week.

1One rule

The model describes. Code computes.

The model describes the lesson: sections, blocks and how they connect. chalk computes every position itself; no tool the model uses accepts a coordinate.

Where a figure can be calculated (a graph, a matrix, a circuit, an engine), chalk’s own code calculates it, so a graph is the real graph. Every board is fact-checked before it’s taught.

2Engines

More than 140 engines.

Some things can’t be explained in a picture; they have to run. So chalk has more than 140 engines, each a small simulator of one subject. The model only names the engine and sets it up. The engine works out the rest.

  • DC circuits

    Solved: every current and voltage, with current flowing faster where there’s more.

  • The heart

    The whole circulation solved beat by beat, valves opening and shutting.

  • Petri nets

    Fire a transition and watch the tokens move.

  • A database that runs

    SQL joins drawn as lines between matching rows.

  • Inheritance

    Tap an allele and the Punnett square fills in.

  • A hash table

    Type a key and watch it hash, probe and land.

  • Recursion

    Factorial, Fibonacci and Hanoi run for real on the call stack.

  • Earthquakes

    P and S waves traced through the Earth, bending as they go.

  • Organic mechanisms

    Curly arrows applied one by one, each next structure worked out.

  • A feedback loop

    Cruise control and a thermostat under on/off, P, PI or PID.

  • Music theory

    A key on the keyboard and the staff, with its scale degrees.

  • Graph search

    BFS, DFS and Dijkstra stepped through on a real graph.

3The drawn board

It draws while it talks.

The model names the figure and its givens, such as a triangle with sides 5 and 7 and a 60° angle. chalk computes the geometry and draws it stroke by stroke, writing each step while its sentence is spoken. Equation lines are checked.

Research on teaching with drawings finds that watching an explanation being drawn, by a visible hand, helps people learn. chalk’s one cursor is that hand.

4Checked

Every line is checked.

Before a line of working goes on the board, chalk works out each side of its “=” with the figure’s own values. If they don’t agree, the line is never written.

A name the figure doesn’t know is left unchecked, never failed. It refuses what is false, not what it cannot read.

5chalk for Mac

On a Mac, it points at the real thing.

When you hold ⌘⌥ and ask, chalk lists what’s on screen: the text it reads and the controls the app itself exposes (macOS accessibility). The model answers by picking a control by name from that list, not by guessing a pixel. chalk then draws the pointer on that control’s real bounds.

Some screens it reads properly: a spreadsheet, a circuit, a LeetCode problem, a chord sheet, a chess or Go game, with real chess and Go engines behind them. And chalk only suggests. It acts only on a ⌘⌥ tap, a click or a spoken “yes”, and never offers to send, pay or delete.

6The illustrations

Drawn in code, in a crayon hand.

chalk’s illustrations are drawn in code, never pictures: flat colour, white streaks where the wax skips, and grain over the lot. Every drawing is seeded, so it comes out the same every time, and every one on this page is drawn that way.

Then the grain boils, stepping through 4 frames on a loop the way a hand-drawn film redraws its line.

And the rest.

  • Circle to ask

    Circle part of a board and “this” means it.

  • One cursor

    The same green buddy builds the board, then walks you through it.

  • Boards are logs

    A board is an append-only log of events, so replay, video and “continue where you stopped” all read the same record.

  • One board, two places

    The web board and the answer on your Mac produce the same document.

  • It comes back

    What you learn comes back days later, then weeks later, and a check a week on shows what stuck.

  • Your own course

    Built from your lectures and past papers, it answers with the pages it used.

  • Guess first

    Draw the line you expect, then chalk runs the real thing over it.

What I learned.

  1. 1

    A model can’t be relied on to do less, so the limits live in code.

  2. 2

    Give the model names, not numbers. It picks the button, the figure, the engine; code finds where.

  3. 3

    If it can be shown, show it: engines, not paragraphs.
