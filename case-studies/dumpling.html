Case study / 003

Building Dumpling: UAT feedback, neatly folded

Testers say what went wrong in their own words. Dumpling folds the duplicates into one ticket, asks for what’s missing, and leaves every call to a person. It was the sole winner of the Pfizer AI Studio Hackathon.

2026Product design and engineering

The problem
In user acceptance testing, one bug comes in as 5 reports in 5 different wordings. Some notes just say “weird”. Ideas get buried, and testers never hear what happened to what they said. Whoever runs the test sorts all of it by hand.
How I framed it
Dumpling proposes; it never approves. It reads every note and suggests what belongs together and what’s missing, and a person makes every call. Its confidence comes in words first and a number second, and the two can never disagree.
The call
Doubtful matches stay outside the group for a person to decide. I ruled out merging whatever looked similar. A report folds into a bug on its own only when Dumpling reads it as the same problem at 68% or above. From 45%, it waits in a separate list for the manager.
The numbers
Sole winner of the Pfizer AI Studio Hackathon, for its “UAT, Untangled” challenge. In the demo test cycle, 21 notes from 6 testers become 17 cases to decide: 14 bug reports fold into 11 bugs, and 5 ideas into 4 themes. Every one still waits for a person.

01

One bug, five reports

User acceptance testing is the last check before something ships. Real people run through a script and say what broke. What they write is honest and messy. Five testers hit the same bug and describe it five ways. One writes three careful sentences; another writes “doesn’t work”. Someone has a good idea halfway through, and it sinks under the bug reports.

Four people pay for it. The tester never hears back, so next time they write less. The manager running the test merges and chases by hand. The developer gets notes too thin to start from. And product never sees the ideas.

So Dumpling had two jobs that pull against each other: do the sorting a manager dreads, without ever taking a decision away from them.

02

Two people, two screens

A tester runs the script one step at a time and picks one of 4 answers: it worked, it didn’t work, something felt off, or I have an idea. Then they write in their own words. Their browser, system, device and step are attached for them, so nobody types “Safari on an iPad” again.

About 850 milliseconds after they stop typing, Dumpling says back what it understood and asks one question, which they can skip with one tap. If someone has already reported something like it, the tester sees that too, with 2 buttons: same as mine, or different. The last thing they read before they move on is that a person reads every note. Later, a tracker shows their note go from sent, to understood, to reviewed, to an outcome.

The manager gets a queue to decide, grouped into bugs, already known, ideas, questions and praise, and notes waiting on testers. They work it one case at a time, with j and k on the keyboard. Each case opens with Dumpling’s read in a single line: how sure it is these reports are one problem, whether there’s enough to act on, and how bad it looks.

03

How sure, in words

A percentage on its own asks for trust it hasn’t earned. So Dumpling leads with words: very likely the same, likely the same, related but probably not the same, probably different, or not sure, your call. The number comes second, and it’s held inside the band its words name. A “probably different” can never carry 90%.

Under the words, the score is plain arithmetic. How alike the wording is counts for 70% of a first pass, and where in the script the two notes came from counts for 30%. Pairs under 0.3 are never compared at all. The rest get a closer read, and the score blends the first pass (40%) with that read (60%). A tester’s own “same as mine” lifts it to at least 90%; their “different” caps it at 40%.

A group is only as sure as its least sure pair. “All of these are one problem” never sounds more certain than the weakest link inside it.

04

Every call is a person’s

Dumpling drafts the ticket: a title, the steps, what was expected, what happened, and a severity. The manager can change all of it. When they change the severity, the control still marks Dumpling’s pick and says plainly that they overruled it. Every judgement can be overruled in one click: not the same, ask the tester first, or change what kind of note it is.

The rules live on the server, not just in the interface. There’s no approve-all button, and the server takes one decision per request, so nothing is approved in a batch and nothing is created without a person. A note too thin to act on isn’t rejected. It’s marked as needing a little more, and the tester gets a question.

Dumpling keeps score of itself, too. Its insights count how often the manager agreed with it, so trust in the tool is earned, not assumed. And once a manager makes a call on a pair, that call outlives any later re-reading of those notes.

05

Witnesses, not duplicates

The usual way treats 5 reports of one bug as noise: keep one, close four. Dumpling treats them as witnesses. Every report stays in the ticket with that tester’s setup and their exact words, because the detail a developer needs could be in any one of them.

When the ticket is created, every tester who saw the bug is told and credited: others saw it too, and their detail is in the ticket. Testers see how many people reported something, never anyone else’s note. The ticket goes to GitHub or Jira, labelled for UAT and with its severity.

The mark says the same thing. It’s a dumpling whose pleats all fold into one point: many notes, one problem.

06

Conclusion

A model is good at the sorting nobody wants to do, and bad at being accountable for it. Dumpling splits the work along that line. It reads, matches, drafts and asks. A person decides, and every decision, theirs or Dumpling’s, is shown for what it is.
